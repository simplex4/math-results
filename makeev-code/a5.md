**No. Counterexamples exist in every dimension \(d\ge 4\).** An additional, sufficiently small, circle-invariant polynomial perturbation of the cubic–quintic construction gives a dimension-independent proof.

I use unit-normalized roots, as required for evaluation on the sphere:

$$
\alpha_{ij}=\frac{e_i-e_j}{\sqrt2}.
$$

## Theorem

For every \(d\ge4\), there is an odd real polynomial \(F:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R\), of degree at most

$$
3d(d+1)-3,
$$

such that no \(A\in O(d)\) and \(b\in\mathbb R^d\) satisfy

$$
F(A\alpha_{ij})=b\cdot A\alpha_{ij}
\qquad(0\le i<j\le d).
$$

Consequently, \(f=F|_{S^{d-1}}\) is a smooth counterexample to the assertion in the question.

The additional polynomial perturbation is important: the proof does **not** assert that the particular quintic in the linked construction works unchanged in every dimension.

---

## 1. Preliminaries

Put

$$
N=d+1,\qquad
V=\mathbf1^\perp\subset\mathbb R^N,\qquad
q_i=e_i-\frac1N\mathbf1,
$$

and

$$
\Pi=I-\frac1N\mathbf1\mathbf1^T.
$$

We regard \(A\) as an orthogonal isomorphism \(V\to\mathbb R^d\), and set \(p_i=Aq_i\). Directly from the definitions,

$$
\sum_i p_i=0,\qquad
p_i\cdot p_j=\delta_{ij}-\frac1N,\qquad
\sum_i p_ip_i^T=I_d.                                      \tag{1}
$$

For an odd function \(G\), let \(Y_G(A)\) be the skew-symmetric matrix

$$
Y_G(A)_{ij}
=
G\!\left(\frac{p_i-p_j}{\sqrt2}\right).
$$

### Lemma 1. Exact edge labels

The desired agreement with a linear functional holds at \(A\) if and only if

$$
\Pi Y_G(A)\Pi=0.                                           \tag{2}
$$

#### Proof

Agreement with a linear functional is equivalent to the existence of vertex potentials \(\rho_i\) such that

$$
Y_G(A)_{ij}=\rho_i-\rho_j.
$$

Indeed, in root coordinates a linear functional gives

$$
c\cdot\alpha_{ij}=\frac{c_i-c_j}{\sqrt2},
$$

and every collection of vertex potentials, after subtracting its mean, arises this way.

A skew matrix \(Y\) of the form \(\rho\mathbf1^T-\mathbf1\rho^T\) satisfies \(\Pi Y\Pi=0\). Conversely, if \(\Pi Y\Pi=0\), expansion of this identity, using skew-symmetry, gives

$$
Y_{ij}=\rho_i-\rho_j,\qquad
\rho_i=\frac1N\sum_jY_{ij}.
$$

∎

We call labels satisfying (2) **exact**.

### Lemma 2. Quadratic coordinates supplied by the roots

The map

$$
\operatorname{Sym}(V)\longrightarrow\mathbb R^{\binom N2},
\qquad
B\longmapsto
\bigl(\alpha_{ij}^TB\alpha_{ij}\bigr)_{i<j}                 \tag{3}
$$

is an isomorphism.

#### Proof

Extend \(B\) to \(\mathbb R^N\) by setting \(B\mathbf1=0\). Let \(W\) be the symmetric matrix with zero diagonal and

$$
W_{ij}=\alpha_{ij}^TB\alpha_{ij}
      =\frac{B_{ii}+B_{jj}}2-B_{ij}.
$$

Thus

$$
\Pi W\Pi=-B.
$$

In particular, (3) is injective. Its domain and codomain both have dimension

$$
n=\frac{d(d+1)}2,
$$

so it is an isomorphism. ∎

We will use elementary semialgebraic dimension theory. Specifically, images of semialgebraic maps are semialgebraic, dimensions add in products, and for a semialgebraic map \(\pi:X\to Y\),

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dim\pi^{-1}(y)\le k\ \text{for all }y
&\implies \dim X\le\dim\pi(X)+k,\\
\dim\pi^{-1}(y)\ge k\ \text{for all }y\in\pi(X)
&\implies \dim X\ge\dim\pi(X)+k.
\end{aligned}                                             \tag{4}
$$

These follow by semialgebraic trivialization and taking dimensions on the finitely many trivializing pieces. A semialgebraic subset of a finite-dimensional real vector space having smaller dimension has empty interior. References are Bochnak–Coste–Roy, *Real Algebraic Geometry*, §§2.8, 9.1 and 9.3; see also the explicit stratification and trivialization statements in Hardt–Lambrechts–Turchin–Volić, §2.1. ([Springer][1])

## 2. A spectral dimension lemma

Fix an orthogonal decomposition

$$
\mathbb R^d=E_1\oplus E_2\oplus E_0
           =\mathbb C\oplus\mathbb C\oplus\mathbb R^{d-4},
$$

and the circle action

$$
h_\theta(z_1,z_2,t)
=
(e^{i\theta}z_1,e^{2i\theta}z_2,t).                        \tag{5}
$$

For \(\lambda_1\in(0,1)\), \(\lambda_2\in(4,5)\), define

$$
D_\lambda
=
\lambda_1I_{E_1}\oplus\lambda_2I_{E_2}
\oplus\operatorname{diag}(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_{d-4}),
\qquad
\mu_j=4j+4.
$$

### Lemma 3. Fibers of a rank-one spectral map

Every fiber of

$$
(A,\lambda_1,\lambda_2,c)
\longmapsto
A^TD_\lambda A+cc^T,
\qquad \|c\|<1,                                          \tag{6}
$$

has dimension at most \(2\).

#### Proof

Write

$$
S=A^TD_\lambda A,\qquad B=S+cc^T.
$$

Both double eigenvalues \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\) of \(S\) remain eigenvalues of \(B\): in each corresponding two-dimensional eigenspace, there is a nonzero vector perpendicular to \(c\). Thus, for a fixed \(B\), there are only finitely many choices of \((\lambda_1,\lambda_2)\).

We next show that, for each such choice, only finitely many \(c\)'s are possible.

Since

$$
0\le cc^T\le \|c\|^2I<I,
$$

the min–max characterization of eigenvalues shows that every ordered eigenvalue of \(B\) lies between the corresponding eigenvalue of \(S\) and that eigenvalue plus \(1\). The distinct eigenvalues of \(S\) are separated by more than \(2\). Consequently, every eigenvalue of \(B\) has multiplicity at most \(2\); a double eigenvalue of \(B\) must be one of the unchanged \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\).

Let \(P_\beta\) denote the orthogonal projection onto the \(\beta\)-eigenspace of \(B\). The rank-one determinant identity gives

$$
\frac{\det(tI-S)}{\det(tI-B)}
=
1+\sum_{\beta\in\operatorname{spec}B}
\frac{\|P_\beta c\|^2}{t-\beta}.                           \tag{7}
$$

The left side is known once \(B,\lambda_1,\lambda_2\) are fixed. Hence all the numbers \(\|P_\beta c\|^2\) are fixed.

If \(\beta\) is a double eigenvalue of \(B\), it is a double eigenvalue of \(S\) as well, so the left side of (7) has no pole there. Therefore \(P_\beta c=0\). All remaining eigenspaces are one-dimensional, and the corresponding components of \(c\) are determined up to sign. Thus there are finitely many possible \(c\)'s.

Now \(S=B-cc^T\) is determined. For fixed \(S,\lambda\), the possible \(A\)'s form a coset of the orthogonal centralizer of \(D_\lambda\), which is

$$
O(2)\times O(2)\times\{\pm1\}^{d-4}.
$$

It has dimension \(2\). The fiber in (6) is therefore contained in a finite union of two-dimensional sets. ∎

## 3. Generic invariant polynomials exclude all nonexceptional rotations

Call a placement \(A\) **exceptional** if some root \(A\alpha_{ij}\) belongs to \(E_1\cup E_2\). Let \(\mathcal E\) be the set of exceptional placements. It is compact.

The reason for this definition is that

$$
h_\theta x=-x\text{ for some }\theta
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
x\in E_1\cup E_2
\qquad(x\in S^{d-1}).                                    \tag{8}
$$

Indeed, the fixed component must vanish; and if both complex components are nonzero, the requirements \(e^{i\theta}=-1\) and \(e^{2i\theta}=-1\) are incompatible.

Put

$$
M=6n-3=3d(d+1)-3,
$$

and let \(\mathcal P\) be the finite-dimensional vector space of odd, \(h_\theta\)-invariant real polynomials of degree at most \(M\).

### Proposition 4. Generic avoidance outside \(\mathcal E\)

The set of \(F\in\mathcal P\) having exact labels at some \(A\notin\mathcal E\) is contained in a semialgebraic subset of \(\mathcal P\) of dimension at most \(\dim\mathcal P-1\). In particular, its complement is dense.

#### Proof

The polynomial map

$$
\Psi(z_1,z_2,t)
=
\bigl(t,\ |z_1|^2,\ |z_2|^2,\
       \operatorname{Re}(z_1^2\overline z_2),\
       \operatorname{Im}(z_1^2\overline z_2)\bigr)          \tag{9}
$$

separates circle orbits. To see this, when \(z_1\ne0\), rotate its phase to a prescribed value; the last two coordinates then determine \(z_2\). When \(z_1=0\), the modulus of \(z_2\) determines its orbit. The fixed coordinates are recorded explicitly.

For \(A\notin\mathcal E\), partition the positive roots into signed circle-orbit classes:

$$
A\alpha_e=\sigma_e h_\theta A\alpha_{e_a},
\qquad \sigma_e\in\{\pm1\},
$$

where \(e_a\) is a chosen representative of class \(a\). By (8), the signs are unambiguous. There are finitely many possible signed partitions.

Fix one such partition, with \(m\) classes, and let \(Z\) be the set of placements realizing it. Equation (9) shows that \(Z\) is semialgebraic. Define

$$
L=
\{c\in V:
c\cdot\alpha_e=\sigma_e\,c\cdot\alpha_{e_a}
\text{ for all }e\},
\qquad \ell=\dim L,
$$

and

$$
\mathscr S=
\{B\in\operatorname{Sym}(V):
\alpha_e^TB\alpha_e
=
\alpha_{e_a}^TB\alpha_{e_a}
\text{ for all }e\}.
$$

Lemma 2 gives

$$
\dim\mathscr S=m.                                        \tag{10}
$$

For \(A\in Z\), \(c\in L\), and \(\lambda\) as in Lemma 3,

$$
A^TD_\lambda A+cc^T\in\mathscr S.                         \tag{11}
$$

The first term has equal quadratic values on signed circle orbits because \(D_\lambda\) commutes with the circle action. The second does because the relevant linear values of \(c\) agree up to sign.

Restrict the auxiliary variable \(c\) to \(L\cap\{\|c\|<1\}\), which still has dimension \(\ell\). Applying Lemma 3 and (4) to (11) gives

$$
\dim Z+2+\ell\le m+2,
$$

hence

$$
\boxed{\dim Z+\ell\le m.}                                \tag{12}
$$

We also need independent interpolation on these \(m\) classes. The \(2m\) points

$$
\Psi(A\alpha_{e_a}),\qquad \Psi(-A\alpha_{e_a})
$$

are all distinct. Choose a linear functional on the target of \(\Psi\) taking distinct values on them. Univariate Lagrange interpolation, followed by composition with this functional and \(\Psi\), prescribes arbitrary values at these \(2m\) points using a polynomial of degree at most \(3(2m-1)\). Taking its odd part preserves prescribed opposite values at antipodal points. Thus

$$
\mathcal P\longrightarrow\mathbb R^m,\qquad
F\longmapsto\bigl(F(A\alpha_{e_a})\bigr)_{a=1}^m           \tag{13}
$$

is surjective for every \(A\in Z\).

Consider the incidence set

$$
\mathcal I=
\left\{(A,c,F)\in Z\times L\times\mathcal P:
F(A\alpha_{e_a})=c\cdot\alpha_{e_a}
\quad(1\le a\le m)\right\}.
$$

By (13), its fibers over \(Z\times L\) are affine spaces of dimension \(\dim\mathcal P-m\). Consequently, (12) yields

$$
\dim\mathcal I
=
\dim Z+\ell+\dim\mathcal P-m
\le\dim\mathcal P.                                       \tag{14}
$$

Every nonempty fiber of the projection \(\mathcal I\to\mathcal P\) contains

$$
\{(h_\theta A,c,F):\theta\in\mathbb R/2\pi\mathbb Z\}.
$$

This is a one-dimensional circle: the action on \(A\) is free. Therefore (4) and (14) imply that the image has dimension at most \(\dim\mathcal P-1\).

Taking the finite union over signed partitions proves the proposition. ∎

The remaining task is to find a nonempty open subset of \(\mathcal P\) avoiding the exceptional placements.

## 4. The cubic and quintic

Write \(z_1=x+iy\), \(z_2=u+iv\), and set

$$
Q=\operatorname{Re}(z_1^2\overline z_2)
  =(x^2-y^2)u+2xyv,
$$

$$
H=|z_1|^2\operatorname{Im}(z_1^2\overline z_2).
                                                               \tag{15}
$$

These are the cubic and quintic used in the linked construction. Both are odd and invariant under (5). 

Let

$$
J(z_1,z_2,t)=(iz_1,2iz_2,0),
\qquad
X_{ij}=p_i\cdot Jp_j,
$$

and define

$$
\Lambda_A(G)
=
\sum_{i<j}X_{ij}
G\!\left(\frac{p_i-p_j}{\sqrt2}\right).                   \tag{16}
$$

Since \(X\mathbf1=0\), exact labels always satisfy

$$
\Lambda_A(G)=0.                                          \tag{17}
$$

### Lemma 5. Cubic identities

For every placement \(A\),

$$
\Lambda_A(Q)=0.                                          \tag{18}
$$

Moreover, let \(x,y,u,v\in\mathbb R^N\) be the first four coordinate columns of the \(p_i\)'s, and let \(P=(x\ y\ u\ v)\). If \(Q\)'s labels are exact, then

$$
G=PS\quad\text{for some symmetric }4\times4\text{ matrix }S, \tag{19}
$$

where

$$
G=
\bigl(2xu+2yv,\ -2yu+2xv,\ x^2-y^2,\ 2xy\bigr).           \tag{20}
$$

Products and squares of columns here and below are componentwise.

#### Proof

The columns of \(P\) are orthonormal and perpendicular to \(\mathbf1\), by (1). Formula (20) therefore gives \(\mathbf1^TG=0\).

For any homogeneous cubic \(C\),

$$
C(p_i-p_j)
=
C(p_i)-C(p_j)-\nabla C(p_i)\cdot p_j
+p_i\cdot\nabla C(p_j).
$$

For \(C=Q\), projection by \(\Pi\) removes the first two terms and gives

$$
\Pi Y_Q(A)\Pi
=
\frac1{2\sqrt2}(PG^T-GP^T).                              \tag{21}
$$

Thus exactness implies \(PG^T=GP^T\). Multiplication by \(P\), using \(P^TP=I_4\), gives (19), with \(S=P^TG=G^TP\) symmetric.

For (18), use the same cubic expansion in (16). The pure terms vanish because \(X\mathbf1=0\). Also, by (1),

$$
\sum_jX_{ij}p_j=-Jp_i,\qquad
\sum_iX_{ij}p_i=Jp_j.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\Lambda_A(Q)
=
\frac1{2\sqrt2}\sum_i\nabla Q(p_i)\cdot Jp_i=0,
$$

the last equality following by differentiating the circle invariance of \(Q\). ∎

## 5. The exceptional placements

The key dimension-independent fact is the following.

### Lemma 6. Nonvanishing on exceptional cubic solutions

If \(A\in\mathcal E\) and \(Q\)'s labels are exact, then

$$
\Lambda_A(H)\ne0.                                        \tag{22}
$$

#### Proof

We first classify the coordinate columns sufficiently to compute (16).

Applying \(h_\theta\) preserves exactness of \(Q\) and the value of \(\Lambda_A(H)\). The same is true of simultaneous complex conjugation

$$
(z_1,z_2,t)\longmapsto(\overline z_1,\overline z_2,t):
$$

it preserves \(Q\), changes the sign of \(H\), and conjugates \(J\) to \(-J\), so the two signs in (16) cancel.

### Case 1: a root lies in \(E_1\)

Rotate by \(h_\theta\) so that this root is the real \(z_1\)-axis. Relabeling its two indices as \(0,1\), we have

$$
x_0=\frac1{\sqrt2},\quad x_1=-\frac1{\sqrt2},
\quad x_i=0\ (i\ne0,1),
$$

and

$$
y_0=y_1=\tau,\qquad u_0=u_1=U,\qquad v_0=v_1=V.
$$

Subtracting rows \(0,1\) in (19), and then using symmetry of \(S\), gives

$$
yv=Vy+\tau v,                                           \tag{23}
$$

$$
yu=a y+b u,\qquad
x^2-y^2=-2b y+m u,\qquad m=U-a,                          \tag{24}
$$

for some real \(a,b\).

For completeness, the row subtraction gives

$$
(S_{xx},S_{xy},S_{xu},S_{xv})=(2U,2V,0,2\tau).
$$

Also \(2xy=2\tau x\), so the \(v\)-row of \(S\) has only its \(x\)-entry nonzero. The \(y\)- and \(u\)-components of (19) now give (24).

At indices \(0,1\), (23) implies \(\tau V=0\). Suppose \(\tau\ne0\). Then \(V=0\), and (23) says that \(v\) is supported where \(y=\tau\). Since \(v\) is a unit column and vanishes at \(0,1\), there is an index \(i\ne0,1\) with \(v_i\ne0\) and \(y_i=\tau\).

Comparing the last equation of (24) at \(0\) and \(i\) gives

$$
m(U-u_i)=\frac12.
$$

In particular, \(U\ne u_i\). Comparing the preceding equation gives \(b=\tau\), and then \(a=0\). Thus

$$
yu=\tau u.
$$

At any index outside \(\{0,1\}\) with \(y_i\ne\tau\), this forces \(u_i=0\), and the last equation of (24) gives

$$
y_i\in\{0,2\tau\}.
$$

Therefore every entry of \(y\) belongs to \(\{0,\tau,2\tau\}\), impossible because \(\sum_i y_i=0\) and \(y_0=\tau\ne0\). Hence

$$
\tau=0.
$$

Now (24) at index \(0\) gives \(mU=1/2\), so \(m,U\ne0\); its other equation gives \(b=0\). Therefore

$$
u=\frac{x^2-y^2}{m},\qquad yu=a y.                       \tag{25}
$$

On the support of \(y\), the value of \(u\) is constant, so (25) makes \(y^2\) constant there. Because \(y\) is a mean-zero unit column, its nonzero entries are equally many plus and minus copies of one number. Finally, (23) says that \(v=V\) on this support, as well as at \(0,1\).

### Case 2: a root lies in \(E_2\)

Rotate so that this root is the real \(z_2\)-axis:

$$
u_0=\frac1{\sqrt2},\quad u_1=-\frac1{\sqrt2},
\quad u_i=0\ (i\ne0,1).
$$

Write \(x_0=x_1=X\), \(y_0=y_1=Y\). Row subtraction in (19) gives

$$
x^2-y^2=2Xx-2Yy.
$$

At index \(0\), this implies \(X^2=Y^2\). Using simultaneous conjugation if necessary, assume \(X=Y\), and put

$$
r=\frac{x-y}{\sqrt2},\qquad
s=\frac{x+y}{\sqrt2},\qquad
\eta=\sqrt2X.
$$

Then

$$
rs=\eta r,\qquad ru=0,\qquad su=\eta u,                  \tag{26}
$$

and \(r_0=r_1=0,\ s_0=s_1=\eta\).

In these coordinates,

$$
Q=2rsu+(s^2-r^2)v.
$$

Using (19), its gradient

$$
(2su-2rv,\ 2ru+2sv,\ 2rs,\ s^2-r^2)
$$

and symmetry of \(S\) give

$$
rv=a r+c v,\qquad sv=e s+f v,                            \tag{27}
$$

$$
s^2-r^2=-2c r+2f s+m v,\qquad m=e-a.                    \tag{28}
$$

To verify that no other components occur in (27), note that the \(u\)-component is already fixed by \(2rs=2\eta r\), while

$$
\langle rv,s\rangle=\langle sv,r\rangle
=\sum_i r_is_iv_i
=\eta\langle r,v\rangle=0.
$$

The coefficients in (28) then follow from symmetry.

We show that \(\eta=0\). Suppose otherwise, and put \(K=\{i:r_i\ne0\}\); on \(K\), \(s_i=\eta\).

If \(c\ne0\), the first equation of (27) forces \(v=0\) outside \(K\). Hence \(sv=\eta v\), so the second gives \(e=0,f=\eta\). Outside \(K\), equation (28) now yields \(s_i\in\{0,2\eta\}\). On \(K\), \(s_i=\eta\). This contradicts \(\sum_i s_i=0\). Thus \(c=0\).

Now \(v=a\) on \(K\). Write \(v_0=v_1=V\). Comparing (28) at an index of \(K\) and at \(0\) gives

$$
r_i^2=m(V-a)>0,
$$

so \(V\ne a\). Comparing \(sv=e s+f v\) at those same indices gives \(f=\eta\), and then \(e=0\). Thus

$$
(s-\eta)v=0.
$$

Equation (28) again shows that every entry of \(s\) belongs to \(\{0,\eta,2\eta\}\), contradicting its zero mean. Therefore \(\eta=0\).

Now \(rs=0\). Multiplying the equations (27) by \(s\) and \(r\), respectively, gives

$$
ce=cf=fa=0.
$$

If \(c\ne0\), then \(e=f=0\); hence \(v=0\) wherever \(s\ne0\), and (28) would give \(s^2=0\) there. Thus \(c=0\). The analogous argument gives \(f=0\). Consequently,

$$
rv=a r,\qquad sv=e s,\qquad s^2-r^2=m v.                 \tag{29}
$$

Here \(m\ne0\), since \(r,s\) are nonzero and have disjoint supports.

It follows that \(r^2\) is constant on its support, and likewise for \(s^2\). Their nonzero entries are balanced plus and minus values. Also \(v\) vanishes outside those two supports.

Finally, applying \(h_{\pi/4}\) changes the complex coordinates to

$$
z_1'=r+is,\qquad z_2'=-v+iu.
$$

Thus the new real \(z_2\)-column is \((r^2-s^2)/m\), and the new imaginary column is zero on the supports of \(r,s\).

### The resulting common form

Both cases have therefore reduced, by transformations preserving \(\Lambda_A(H)\), to the following form. There are disjoint index sets \(K,L\), of even cardinalities \(k,l\ge2\), such that

$$
x_i=
\begin{cases}
\pm k^{-1/2},&i\in K,\\
0,&i\notin K,
\end{cases}
\qquad
y_i=
\begin{cases}
\pm l^{-1/2},&i\in L,\\
0,&i\notin L,
\end{cases}                                             \tag{30}
$$

with balanced signs, and

$$
u=\frac{x^2-y^2}{m},\qquad
v_i=\omega\quad(i\in K\cup L).                           \tag{31}
$$

Since \(u\) is a unit column,

$$
m^2=\frac1k+\frac1l.                                    \tag{32}
$$

We now compute the obstruction in this form. Set

$$
U=\frac1{km},\qquad V=-\frac1{lm};
$$

then \(u=U\) on \(K\), \(u=V\) on \(L\), and \(U-V=m\).

For an edge, write \(\Delta x=x_i-x_j\), and similarly for the other coordinates. Directly from (15),

$$
H\!\left(\frac{p_i-p_j}{\sqrt2}\right)
=
\frac{(\Delta x^2+\Delta y^2)
\bigl(2\Delta x\Delta y\Delta u
-(\Delta x^2-\Delta y^2)\Delta v\bigr)}
{4\sqrt2},                                             \tag{33}
$$

while

$$
X_{ij}
=
-x_i y_j+y_i x_j-2u_i v_j+2v_i u_j.                     \tag{34}
$$

Let \(O\) be the complement of \(K\cup L\). Edges internal to \(K,L,O\) contribute zero. For edges between \(K\) and \(L\), equations (30)–(34), with cancellation of the balanced signs, give

$$
\sum_{i\in K,\ j\in L}
X_{ij}H\!\left(\frac{p_i-p_j}{\sqrt2}\right)
=
\frac{m^3}{2\sqrt2}.                                    \tag{35}
$$

The edges from \(K\cup L\) to \(O\) contribute

$$
\frac1{2\sqrt2\,m}
\left(\frac1{k^2}+\frac1{l^2}\right)
\sum_{j\in O}v_j(\omega-v_j).                           \tag{36}
$$

These sums count each unordered edge once; the product in (16) is unchanged when its orientation is reversed.

Because \(v\) has zero mean and norm \(1\),

$$
\sum_{j\in O}v_j=-(k+l)\omega,\qquad
\sum_{j\in O}v_j^2=1-(k+l)\omega^2.
$$

Thus the final sum in (36) equals \(-1\). Combining (32), (35) and (36), we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Lambda_A(H)
&=
\frac1{2\sqrt2}
\left[m^3-\frac{k^{-2}+l^{-2}}m\right]\\
&=
\boxed{\frac1{\sqrt2\,klm}},
\end{aligned}                                           \tag{37}
$$

which is nonzero. This proves the lemma. ∎

## 6. Completion of the construction

We first claim that, for every sufficiently small nonzero \(\epsilon\),

$$
F_\epsilon=Q+\epsilon H                                 \tag{38}
$$

has no exact labels at any exceptional placement.

Otherwise, there would be nonzero \(\epsilon_j\to0\) and \(A_j\in\mathcal E\) such that \(Y_{F_{\epsilon_j}}(A_j)\) is exact. By (17) and (18),

$$
0=\Lambda_{A_j}(F_{\epsilon_j})
=\epsilon_j\Lambda_{A_j}(H),
$$

so \(\Lambda_{A_j}(H)=0\).

Pass to a convergent subsequence \(A_j\to A_\infty\in\mathcal E\). Exactness is closed by Lemma 1, so \(Q\)'s labels at \(A_\infty\) are exact. Continuity also gives

$$
\Lambda_{A_\infty}(H)=0,
$$

contradicting Lemma 6. This proves the claim.

Fix such an \(\epsilon\). Since \(\mathcal E\) is compact,

$$
\min_{A\in\mathcal E}
\|\Pi Y_{F_\epsilon}(A)\Pi\|>0.
$$

Therefore all polynomials in a sufficiently small neighborhood of \(F_\epsilon\) in \(\mathcal P\) also avoid exact labels on \(\mathcal E\).

By Proposition 4, this neighborhood contains an \(F\) avoiding exact labels at every placement outside \(\mathcal E\) as well. Thus \(F\) has no exact labels at any orthogonal placement. Lemma 1 proves the theorem.

In particular, the counterexample can be chosen in the form

$$
\boxed{
F
=
\operatorname{Re}(z_1^2\overline z_2)
+\epsilon |z_1|^2\operatorname{Im}(z_1^2\overline z_2)
+R,
}
$$

where \(\epsilon\ne0\) is sufficiently small and \(R\) is an arbitrarily small, suitably chosen odd circle-invariant polynomial of degree at most

$$
3d(d+1)-3.
$$

This gives a counterexample for **every \(d\ge4\)**, and therefore for every \(d>5\) in the question. ∎
