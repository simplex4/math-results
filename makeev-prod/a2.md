## Overall assessment

**Yes, the proof can be made substantially shorter and more conceptual, but the main algebraic lemma in dimension (4) does not appear to follow from an existing result.** The literature can replace much of the background, graph-linear algebra, degree theory, and symmetry discussion. It does **not** appear to replace the classification and nonvanishing calculation in Lemma 4.

The most effective simplification is actually a combination of citation and reorganization:

1. Cite Kuperberg for the convex-geometric equivalence and test-bundle history.
2. Cite combinatorial Hodge theory for the exact-label/cycle-space decomposition.
3. Combine the cubic theorem and the global cubic annihilation lemma into a single gradient identity.
4. Replace the final sequential perturbation argument by a short abstract compactness lemma.
5. Move the long multiplication-table calculation to an appendix or supplement.

Also, once part 2 is included, part 1’s opening assertion that the unrestricted problem remains open should be removed or explicitly marked as the historical status before the counterexample.

## 1. The background can mostly be replaced by Kuperberg

Kuperberg already establishes:

* the equivalence between constant-width bodies and odd functions on the sphere;
* the equivalence between circumscription by the dual difference body of a simplex and linearity of an odd function on a rotated (A_d) root system;
* the test-bundle formulation;
* the nonzero Euler-class argument in dimension (3);
* the vanishing of that primary obstruction in dimensions (d\geq4).

Thus the historical and bundle-theoretic portion can be reduced to approximately one paragraph:

> Kuperberg showed that Makeev’s constant-width-body conjecture is equivalent to the assertion that every continuous odd function on (S^{d-1}) agrees with a linear function on a rotated (A_d) root system. He proved the assertion in dimension (3) by a nonzero Euler-class calculation and showed that the same Euler-class obstruction vanishes in dimensions (d\geq4), leaving the higher-dimensional geometric question undecided.

That accurately summarizes the prior literature without reconstructing the bundle. ([NYJM][1])

The explicit normalized coordinates
[
u_{ij}=\frac{e_i-e_j}{\sqrt2}
]
should still be retained, because all subsequent calculations depend on that normalization.

---

## 2. The exact-label lemma is standard graph Hodge theory

The exact-label space is the cut or gradient space of the complete graph (K_{d+1}). The space
[
\mathcal C={X:X^T=-X,\ X\mathbf1=0}
]
is its orthogonal complement, the divergence-free cycle space.

In cochain language:

* (c) is a (0)-cochain;
* (y_{ij}=(c_i-c_j)/\sqrt2) is its coboundary;
* the triangle sum
  [
  y_{ij}+y_{jk}+y_{ki}
  ]
  is the combinatorial curl;
* because the clique complex of the complete graph is a simplex and has (H^1=0), curl-free (1)-cochains are exact;
* the orthogonal complement of the gradients is the divergence-free space.

This is precisely the complete-graph specialization of the combinatorial Hodge/Helmholtz decomposition. Jiang–Lim–Yao–Ye explicitly formulate gradient edge flows, divergence-free flows, triangle curl, and the fact that on a complete graph triangular consistency implies global consistency. ([arXiv][2])

I would nevertheless keep a four-line direct proof, because the formula
[
c_i=\sqrt2,y_{i0}
]
makes the result completely transparent. The literature citation should replace the dimension-count discussion and supply terminology, not make the paper less self-contained.

Most importantly, **Part 2 should not repeat the entire lemma**. Its matrix condition
[
\Pi Y\Pi=0
]
is merely the same decomposition in the (m=5) model. The two current versions occupy substantial space.  

A concise combined statement would be:

> For a skew edge matrix (Y), the following are equivalent:
> [
> Y_{ij}=\rho_i-\rho_j,\qquad
> Y_{ij}+Y_{jk}+Y_{ki}=0,\qquad
> \Pi Y\Pi=0.
> ]
> Equivalently, (Y) is orthogonal to every skew matrix (X) with (X\mathbf1=0).

## 3. One gradient identity replaces two major arguments

This is the most important simplification.

For a cubic (P), define
[
\Psi_P(A)=\langle P,A\cdot p\rangle,
\qquad
p(x)=\sum_{i=0}^d x_i^3.
]
Identify
[
T_A SO(V)={AX:X\in\mathfrak{so}(V)}
]
using the curve (Ae^{tX}). The existing identity (2.2) immediately gives
[
\boxed{
d\Psi_P(A)[AX]
==============

2\sqrt2,
\big\langle \mathcal T_P(A),X\big\rangle_{\mathcal C}.
}
\tag{G}
]
Indeed,
[
\begin{aligned}
d\Psi_P(A)[AX]
&=\langle A^{-1}!\cdot P,X\cdot p\rangle\
&=2\sqrt2\sum_{i<j}X_{ij}P(Au_{ij})\
&=2\sqrt2\langle X,\mathcal T_P(A)\rangle.
\end{aligned}
]

Thus the cubic test map is, up to the constant (2\sqrt2), the gradient of the height function (\Psi_P) on (SO(V)). This is already implicit in the present cubic calculation. 

### The cubic theorem then becomes three lines

Because (SO(V)) is compact, (\Psi_P) has a critical point (A). By (G),
[
\mathcal T_P(A)=0.
]
The exact-label lemma supplies the required linear function. A linear summand is already exact for every (A).

The tensor-diagonalization literature provides a natural citation for this viewpoint: Martin and Van Loan study orthogonal transformations maximizing sums of diagonal entries of a third-order tensor, which is closely analogous to maximizing (\langle P,A\cdot p\rangle). Their result does not imply the special (A_d) identity (G), so (G) must remain in the paper, but their work gives appropriate context and terminology. ([SIAM][3])

### Part 2, Lemma 3, becomes a direct corollary

Let
[
\Psi_Q(g)=\langle Q\circ g,P_3\rangle.
]
Since
[
Q\circ h_\theta=Q,
]
one has
[
\Psi_Q(h_\theta g)=\Psi_Q(g).
]
Set
[
K=g^{-1}Jg.
]
Because (h_\theta g=ge^{\theta K}), differentiating and applying (G) gives
[
\begin{aligned}
0
&=\left.\frac d{d\theta}\right|*{\theta=0}\Psi_Q(h*\theta g)\
&=2\sqrt2\sum_{i<j}
\langle q_i,Kq_j\rangle Q(gu_{ij})\
&=2\sqrt2,\Lambda_g(Y_Q(g)).
\end{aligned}
]
That proves the first half of Lemma 3. The same sentence applies to (\operatorname{Im}W).

This eliminates the second tensor-polarization and skew-adjointness calculation currently appearing in part 2. 

It also makes the geometry much clearer:

* exact cubic labels are precisely the critical points of a height function on the orbit (SO(d)\cdot p);
* the functional (\Lambda_g) arises from a continuous symmetry of that height function.

This is more informative than presenting Lemma 3 as a separate computational coincidence.

## 4. The (C^0)-stability proof can cite local degree theory

The calculation
[
D\mathcal T_p(I)=-\frac3{\sqrt2}I
]
must remain. It is the substantive local fact. 

After that, the rest can be compressed to:

> Since (0) is a nondegenerate zero of (\mathcal T_p), its local Brouwer degree is (\pm1). Nonzero local degree persists under sufficiently small uniform perturbations. Since
> [
> |\mathcal T_f-\mathcal T_p|*\infty
> \leq\sqrt{\binom{d+1}{2}}|f-p|*\infty,
> ]
> every sufficiently small (C^0) perturbation of (p) has a zero of its test map.

The current boundary-ball and straight-line homotopy argument is correct but is essentially the proof of this standard local-degree stability theorem.  Brouwer degree is specifically designed to obtain existence and persistence under uniform rather than differentiable perturbations. ([Springer][4])

An inverse-function-theorem citation alone would **not** be an adequate replacement: it would naturally give stability under (C^1) perturbations of the test map, whereas the theorem asserts stability under (C^0) perturbations of (f).

## 5. The final perturbation argument has a short abstract form

The sequential compactness proof in part 2 is valid, but it can be replaced by the following reusable lemma.

### Compact obstruction lemma

Let (M) be compact, let (E) be finite-dimensional, and let
[
F_0,F_1:M\longrightarrow E
]
be continuous. Suppose that there is a continuous family
[
\lambda_x\in E^*
]
such that

[
\lambda_x(F_0(x))=0
\quad\text{for every }x\in M,
\tag{A}
]
and
[
F_0(x)=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\lambda_x(F_1(x))\ne0.
\tag{B}
]

Then there is (\varepsilon_0>0) such that
[
F_0(x)+\varepsilon F_1(x)\ne0
]
for every (x\in M) and every (0<|\varepsilon|<\varepsilon_0).

#### Proof

Let (Z=F_0^{-1}(0)). By compactness and (B), there is a neighborhood (U) of (Z) on which
[
\lambda_x(F_1(x))\ne0.
]
On the compact set (M\setminus U),
[
a:=\min_{M\setminus U}|F_0|>0.
]
Also let (B=\max_M|F_1|). For (|\varepsilon|<a/B), the sum cannot vanish on (M\setminus U). If it vanished at (x\in U), then applying (\lambda_x) and using (A) would give
[
0=\varepsilon\lambda_x(F_1(x)),
]
a contradiction. ∎

Apply this with
[
M=SO(4),\qquad
F_0=\mathcal T_Q,\qquad
F_1=\mathcal T_H,
]
and with (\lambda_g) induced by (\Lambda_g). Lemma 3 is condition (A), and Lemma 4 is condition (B). This replaces all of Section 5 by one paragraph. The current sequential version appears at the end of part 2. 

Karasev’s counterexample to a different Knaster-like conjecture of Makeev uses a genuinely similar pattern: introduce small geometric parameters, assume solutions persist, pass by compactness to a limiting configuration, and obtain an infinitesimal condition deliberately violated by the chosen function. It is worth citing as methodological precedent, although the actual obstruction is different. ([arXiv][5])

## 6. The long algebraic Lemma 4 does not seem to have a literature shortcut

This is the part that remains.

Lemma 4 proves the highly specific statement
[
\mathcal T_Q(g)=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\Lambda_g(Y_H(g))\ne0
]
for the particular cubic (Q), quintic (H), simplex, and functional (\Lambda_g). 

The following standard tools do **not** replace it:

* generic transversality;
* Palais-type symmetric criticality;
* the Euler-class calculation;
* general forced-symmetry-breaking theory;
* ordinary representation-theoretic disjointness;
* standard tensor diagonalization.

Those theories explain why a transverse perturbation is plausible, but they do not prove that this explicit quintic is nonzero at **every** cubic zero.

### Comparison with Chen’s representation-theoretic argument

Chen’s 2026 argument for the regular dodecahedron is the closest recent model. It uses an (A_5)-equivariant evaluation map and the fact that degree-(3) harmonics and linear functions have no common irreducible (A_5)-summands. Schur’s lemma therefore forces both sides of a putative equality to vanish. ([arXiv][6])

That shortcut is unavailable for the (A_4) simplex. Let (V_4) denote the standard (4)-dimensional representation of (A_5). A character calculation using the same (A_5) table gives
[
\mathcal E_{\mathrm{exact}}\cong V_4,
]
and
[
\mathcal C
\cong\Lambda^2V_4
\cong V_3\oplus V_3'.
]
Meanwhile,
[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal H_3(\mathbb R^4)
&\cong \operatorname{Sym}^3(V_4)\ominus V_4\
&\cong
\mathbf1\oplus V_3\oplus V_3'
\oplus V_4\oplus V_5.
\end{aligned}
]
Thus every representation type occurring in the exact-label and cycle spaces already occurs in the harmonic cubics. There is no irreducible-component mismatch that can force the cycle defect to vanish.

Representation theory can still improve the exposition:

* the tangent space to the orbit of (p) is
  [
  T_p(SO(4)\cdot p)\cong V_3\oplus V_3';
  ]
* cubic exactness means (Q\circ g) lies in its normal space
  [
  \mathbf1\oplus V_4\oplus V_5;
  ]
* Lemma 4 is then a statement about a particular quintic functional on that critical locus.

But the critical locus still has to be classified, or the nonvanishing still has to be proved by some equivalent calculation. I found no published result that performs that classification.

## 7. How to present Lemma 4 more economically

Although it cannot safely be replaced by a citation, it can be isolated much better.

### Main text

State an algebraic proposition:

> **Algebraic transversality proposition.**
> If the (Q)-labels of an oriented regular (4)-simplex are exact, then the associated quintic obstruction (\Lambda_g(H)) is nonzero.

Then give only the roadmap:

1. choose the orthogonal basis
   [
   \mathbf1,a,b,\bar a,\bar b;
   ]
2. exactness yields
   [
   s=0,\qquad m\in\mathbb R,\qquad4u=\bar n;
   ]
3. associativity of coordinatewise multiplication yields equations (E1)–(E8);
4. those equations give the three possible cases;
5. the obstruction equals
   [
   m(180m^2+105|n|^2-4400t^2-128)-3520\ell t^2,
   ]
   which is nonzero in all three cases.

The current proof of this proposition occupies most of part 2. 

### Appendix or supplement

Move the following there:

* all coefficients of the multiplication table;
* the extraction of E1–E8 from individual associativity identities;
* the full resultant calculation;
* the raw wedge expansion (54);
* the expression (\mathcal B_{\mathrm{raw}});
* the exact symbolic verification script.

That does not weaken the proof. It separates the conceptual argument from its algebraic certificate.

A useful intermediate presentation would retain the multiplication table and the final three-case table in the paper, while relegating the coefficient-by-coefficient verifications to the appendix.

## 8. Parts that are already simpler than any useful citation

### The zonal theorem

The two-block construction is short, explicit, and more readable than invoking orbit or design terminology. It should remain essentially unchanged. 

### The cyclic simplex

The vectors
[
q_j=\sqrt{\frac25}(\zeta^j,\zeta^{2j})
]
can be described as a harmonic-frame or Fourier-frame realization of a regular simplex, but the roots-of-unity Gram calculation is shorter than explaining that terminology. A citation would be optional background rather than a simplification.

### The harmonic refinement

One could cite the Fischer decomposition
[
\mathcal P_5=\mathcal H_5\oplus |x|^2\mathcal P_3,
]
but the displayed Laplacian calculation already obtains the coefficient (3/5) in two lines. The direct calculation should stay.

## Recommended final architecture

A polished paper could have the following structure:

1. **Background and test map.**
   Cite Kuperberg; give the normalized roots and one concise Hodge/cut-cycle lemma.

2. **The cubic gradient identity.**
   Prove (G) once. Deduce the cubic theorem, the global annihilation identity, and the local derivative at (p).

3. **Positive results.**
   State the (C^0)-stability theorem using local degree and give the zonal construction.

4. **The (d=4) counterexample.**
   Introduce the cyclic simplex, (Q), (H), and the compact obstruction lemma.

5. **Algebraic transversality proposition.**
   Give its short roadmap and three final cases; place the computational proof in an appendix.

6. **Harmonic degree-((3,5)) corollary.**

With this organization, the conceptual main proof could be reduced by roughly a third, and by considerably more if the detailed algebra is placed in an appendix. The essential new mathematical content would then be unmistakable: **the gradient identity for cubics and the explicit algebraic transversality proposition in dimension (4)**.
