# Negative answer in dimension (4)

The statement is **false for (d=4)**.

The counterexample below appears to be new: Kuperberg proved the (d=3) case and found that the homological obstruction vanishes in higher dimensions, leaving the geometric question inconclusive; a paper posted on July 11, 2026 still states the regular-(A_d) assertion as Makeev’s conjecture. ([arXiv][1]) Because the argument would therefore refute an open conjecture, it deserves independent checking. The proof is self-contained, and the longer polynomial identities have also been checked by exact symbolic algebra.

As in the attachment, the roots must be normalized:
[
u_{ij}=\frac{e_i-e_j}{\sqrt2}.
]
The exact-edge-label criterion from the attachment is used below, as is its cubic variational identity.   

---

## Theorem

Identify
[
\mathbb R^4\simeq\mathbb C^2,
\qquad
S^3={(z_1,z_2):|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2=1}.
]
Put
[
W(z)=z_1^2\overline {z_2},
\qquad
Q(z)=\operatorname{Re}W(z),
\qquad
H(z)=|z_1|^2\operatorname{Im}W(z).
]

There exists (\varepsilon _0>0) such that, for every real
[
0<|\varepsilon|<\varepsilon *0,
]
the smooth odd function
[
\boxed{;
f*\varepsilon(z_1,z_2)
======================

\operatorname{Re}(z_1^2\overline {z_2})
+
\varepsilon |z_1|^2\operatorname{Im}(z_1^2\overline {z_2})
;}
\tag{1}
]
has the following property:

> There are no (A\in SO(4)) and (b\in\mathbb R^4) such that
> [
> f_\varepsilon(Au_{ij})=b\cdot Au_{ij}
> \qquad(0\leq i<j\leq4).
> ]

Thus the answer to the question is **no already for (d=4)**.

---

# Proof

## 1. A cyclic model of the (A_4) roots

Let
[
\zeta=e^{2\pi i/5}
]
and define five vectors in (\mathbb C^2) by
[
q_j=\sqrt{\frac25},(\zeta^j,\zeta^{2j}),
\qquad j=0,\ldots,4.
\tag{2}
]

### Lemma 1 — The (q_j) form a regular (4)-simplex

They satisfy
[
\sum_{j=0}^4q_j=0
]
and
[
\langle q_i,q_j\rangle
======================

\begin{cases}
\frac45,&i=j,[1mm]
-\frac15,&i\ne j.
\end{cases}
\tag{3}
]
Consequently
[
u_{ij}:=\frac{q_i-q_j}{\sqrt2}
\qquad (i\ne j)
\tag{4}
]
are unit vectors forming an (A_4) root system.

#### Proof

The sum vanishes because
[
\sum_{j=0}^4\zeta^j=\sum_{j=0}^4\zeta^{2j}=0.
]
Moreover,
[
\langle q_i,q_j\rangle
======================

\frac25\operatorname{Re}
\left(\zeta^{i-j}+\zeta^{2(i-j)}\right).
]
For (i=j) this equals (4/5). For (i\ne j),
[
\cos\frac{2\pi(i-j)}5+\cos\frac{4\pi(i-j)}5=-\frac12,
]
so the inner product is (-1/5). Hence
[
|q_i-q_j|^2=\frac45+\frac45-2\left(-\frac15\right)=2,
]
which proves (4) consists of unit vectors.

The isometry
[
e_j-\frac15\mathbf1\longmapsto q_j
]
identifies these roots with the usual normalized (A_4) roots. An odd permutation of the five vertices reverses orientation while leaving the unoriented root system invariant, so the isometry may be chosen orientation-preserving. ∎

---

## 2. Exact edge labels

For an odd function (F:S^3\to\mathbb R) and (g\in SO(4)), define the skew matrix
[
Y_F(g)*{ij}=F(gu*{ij}),
\qquad
Y_F(g)*{ji}=-Y_F(g)*{ij}.
\tag{5}
]
Let
[
\Pi=I-\frac15\mathbf1\mathbf1^{T}.
]

### Lemma 2 — Cycle-matrix criterion

The following are equivalent:

1. there exists (c\in\mathbb R^4) such that
   [
   Y_F(g)*{ij}=c\cdot u*{ij};
   ]
2. there exist real numbers (\rho_i) such that
   [
   Y_F(g)_{ij}=\rho_i-\rho_j;
   ]
3. one has
   [
   \Pi Y_F(g)\Pi=0.
   \tag{6}
   ]

#### Proof

The equivalence of the first two assertions follows from the simplex frame. Indeed, after subtracting its mean we may suppose (\sum_i\rho_i=0), and then
[
c=\sqrt2\sum_i\rho_iq_i
]
satisfies
[
c\cdot q_j=\sqrt2,\rho_j,
\qquad
c\cdot u_{ij}=\rho_i-\rho_j.
]

If (Y_{ij}=\rho_i-\rho_j), then
[
Y=\rho\mathbf1^T-\mathbf1\rho^T,
]
and hence (\Pi Y\Pi=0).

Conversely, suppose (\Pi Y\Pi=0), and put
[
\rho_i=\frac15\sum_jY_{ij}.
]
Writing (J_0=\frac15\mathbf1\mathbf1^T), skew-symmetry gives (J_0YJ_0=0), and
[
0=(I-J_0)Y(I-J_0)
=Y-J_0Y-YJ_0.
]
Now
[
(J_0Y)*{ij}=-\rho_j,
\qquad
(YJ_0)*{ij}=\rho_i,
]
so
[
Y_{ij}=\rho_i-\rho_j.
]
∎

We call such a label matrix **exact**.

## 3. A cubic annihilating functional

Consider the one-parameter subgroup
[
h_\theta(z_1,z_2)
=================

(e^{i\theta}z_1,e^{2i\theta}z_2).
\tag{7}
]
Its infinitesimal generator is the skew operator
[
J(z_1,z_2)=(iz_1,2iz_2).
\tag{8}
]
Both (Q) and (\operatorname{Im}W) are invariant under (h_\theta).

For (g\in SO(4)), write
[
p_i=gq_i
]
and define
[
X(g)*{ij}=\langle p_i,Jp_j\rangle.
\tag{9}
]
Since (J) is skew, (X(g)) is skew. Also,
[
\sum_jX(g)*{ij}
===============

\left\langle p_i,J\sum_jp_j\right\rangle
=0.
\tag{10}
]
Define
[
\Lambda_g(y)=\sum_{i<j}X(g)*{ij}y*{ij}.
\tag{11}
]

By (10), (\Lambda_g) annihilates every exact edge label:
[
\Lambda_g\bigl((\rho_i-\rho_j)_{ij}\bigr)=0.
\tag{12}
]

### Lemma 3 — Global cubic annihilation

For every (g\in SO(4)),
[
\Lambda_g\bigl((Q(gu_{ij}))*{ij}\bigr)=0
\tag{13}
]
and likewise
[
\Lambda_g\bigl((\operatorname{Im}W(gu*{ij}))_{ij}\bigr)=0.
\tag{14}
]

#### Proof

Let
[
P_3(x)=\sum_{i=0}^4\langle q_i,x\rangle^3.
]
For a homogeneous cubic (C) and (K\in\mathfrak {so}(4)), the cubic identity proved in the attachment becomes
[
\bigl\langle C,K\cdot P_3\bigr\rangle
=====================================

2\sqrt2\sum_{i<j}
\langle q_i,Kq_j\rangle C(u_{ij}),
\tag{15}
]
where the inner product is the invariant inner product on symmetric cubic tensors and
[
(K\cdot C)(x)=-dC_x(Kx).
]
Indeed, if (T_C) is the symmetric trilinear polarization of (C), both sides of (15) are
[
-3\sum_{i,j}K_{ij}(T_C)_{iij}.
]

Take
[
K=g^{-1}Jg,
\qquad
C=Q\circ g.
]
Then
[
\langle q_i,Kq_j\rangle
=======================

\langle p_i,Jp_j\rangle=X(g)*{ij}.
]
Moreover,
[
K\cdot(Q\circ g)=(J\cdot Q)\circ g=0
]
because (Q) is invariant under (h*\theta). Since infinitesimal orthogonal actions are skew-adjoint,
[
\bigl\langle Q\circ g,K\cdot P_3\bigr\rangle
============================================

-\bigl\langle K\cdot(Q\circ g),P_3\bigr\rangle
=0.
]
Equation (15) gives (13). The same proof applies to (\operatorname{Im}W). ∎

The point is that (\Lambda_g) vanishes on the cubic part for **every** (g), not just when the cubic labels are exact.

# 4. The transversality lemma

The main new ingredient is the following.

### Lemma 4

Suppose
[
\bigl(Q(gu_{ij})\bigr)*{i<j}
]
is exact. Then
[
\boxed{;
\Lambda_g\bigl((H(gu*{ij}))_{i<j}\bigr)\ne0.
;}
\tag{16}
]

We now prove this algebraically.

---

## 4.1. Frame coordinates

Write
[
p_i=(a_i,b_i)\in\mathbb C^2
]
and let
[
a=(a_0,\ldots,a_4),\qquad
b=(b_0,\ldots,b_4)\in\mathbb C^5.
]

Because the five (p_i) form the regular simplex (3), the four real coordinate columns of the (5\times4) matrix with rows (p_i) are orthonormal and perpendicular to (\mathbf1). It follows that
[
\sum_i a_i=\sum_i b_i=0,
\tag{17}
]
[
\sum_i a_i^2=\sum_i b_i^2
=\sum_i a_ib_i
=\sum_i a_i\overline {b_i}=0,
\tag{18}
]
[
\sum_i|a_i|^2=\sum_i|b_i|^2=2,
\tag{19}
]
and, coordinatewise,
[
|a_i|^2+|b_i|^2=\frac45.
\tag{20}
]

Consequently
[
\mathbf1,a,b,\bar a,\bar b
\tag{21}
]
are mutually orthogonal for the Hermitian inner product on (\mathbb C^5), with squared norms (5,2,2,2,2), respectively. Thus every (x\in\mathbb C^5) expands as
[
x=
\frac{\sum_i x_i}{5}\mathbf1
+\frac12\Bigl(
\langle a,x\rangle a
+\langle b,x\rangle b
+\langle\bar a,x\rangle\bar a
+\langle\bar b,x\rangle\bar b
\Bigr).
\tag{22}
]

All products of vectors below are coordinatewise. For example,
[
a^2=(a_i^2)_i,\qquad
a\bar b=(a_i\bar b_i)*i.
]
For (u,v\in\mathbb C^5), put
[
(u\wedge v)*{ij}=u_iv_j-v_iu_j.
\tag{23}
]

---

## 4.2. The cycle equations for the cubic (Q)

Ignoring the harmless nonzero factor (1/(2\sqrt2)), the complex edge values of (W) are
[
\widetilde Y_{ij}
=================

(a_i-a_j)^2(\bar b_i-\bar b_j).
\tag{24}
]
Modulo an exact matrix, expansion of (24) gives
[
\Pi\widetilde Y\Pi
==================

-a^2\wedge\bar b
+2a\wedge(a\bar b).
\tag{25}
]
Indeed, the term
[
a_i^2\bar b_i-a_j^2\bar b_j
]
is exact; the remaining terms are precisely the right-hand side of (25).

Define
[
s=\frac12\sum_i|a_i|^2a_i,
\qquad
\lambda=\frac12\sum_i|a_i|^2\bar b_i,
\tag{26}
]
[
m=\sum_i a_i^2\bar b_i,
\qquad
n=\sum_i a_i^3,
\qquad
u=\frac12\sum_i a_i\bar b_i^2,
\qquad
\ell=\frac12\sum_i a_i^2b_i.
\tag{27}
]
Formula (22) gives
[
a^2
===

sa+\frac m2b+\frac n2\bar a+\ell\bar b
\tag{28}
]
and
[
a\bar b
=======

\lambda a+ub+\frac m2\bar a-s\bar b.
\tag{29}
]
Substitution into (25) yields
[
\begin{aligned}
\omega:=\Pi\widetilde Y\Pi
={}&2u,a\wedge b
+m,a\wedge\bar a
-3s,a\wedge\bar b\
&-\frac m2b\wedge\bar b
-\frac n2\bar a\wedge\bar b.
\end{aligned}
\tag{30}
]

Exactness of the real (Q)-labels is precisely
[
\operatorname{Re}\omega=0.
]
Because (a,b,\bar a,\bar b) are a complex basis of (\mathbf1^\perp_\mathbb C), their six wedges are linearly independent. Comparing (30) with its complex conjugate gives
[
\boxed{;
s=0,\qquad m\in\mathbb R,\qquad 4u=\bar n.
;}
\tag{31}
]

---

## 4.3. The coordinatewise multiplication table

Replacing (g) by (h_\theta g) does not change any (Q)- or (H)-value, does not change (X(g)), and sends
[
\lambda\longmapsto e^{-2i\theta}\lambda.
]
We may therefore suppose
[
\lambda=t\geq0.
\tag{32}
]

In addition to (m,n,\ell), define
[
v=\sum_i a_ib_i^2,
\qquad
k=\sum_i b_i^3.
\tag{33}
]

Using (20), (22), and (31), all coordinatewise products of the four basis vectors are:

[
\begin{aligned}
a\bar a&=\frac25\mathbf1+t b+t\bar b,\
b\bar b&=\frac25\mathbf1-t b-t\bar b,[1mm]
a^2&=\frac m2b+\frac n2\bar a+\ell\bar b,\
\bar a^2&=\frac m2\bar b+\frac{\bar n}{2}a+\bar\ell b,[1mm]
a\bar b&=t a+\frac{\bar n}{4}b+\frac m2\bar a,\
\bar a b&=t\bar a+\frac n4\bar b+\frac m2a,[1mm]
ab&=t a+\ell\bar a+\frac v2\bar b,\
\bar a\bar b&=t\bar a+\bar\ell a+\frac{\bar v}{2}b,[1mm]
b^2&=\frac n4a-tb+\frac v2\bar a+\frac k2\bar b,\
\bar b^2&=\frac{\bar n}{4}\bar a-t\bar b
+\frac{\bar v}{2}a+\frac{\bar k}{2}b.
\end{aligned}
\tag{34}
]

For example, the coefficient of (b) in (a\bar a) is
[
\frac12\sum_i\bar b_i|a_i|^2=t,
]
while the coefficient of (\bar b) is its conjugate. The second identity follows from (20). Every other coefficient in (34) is obtained in exactly the same way from (22).

Coordinatewise multiplication is associative. Comparing coefficients in (34) gives the following necessary equations:

[
3mn=4tv,
\tag{E1}
]
[
\bar n,v+16\ell t+8mt=0,
\tag{E2}
]
[
-2km+24\ell t-n^2=0,
\tag{E3}
]
[
-kt+\ell m+4t^2=0,
\tag{E4}
]
[
\bar n,k+mn+8tv=0,
\tag{E5}
]
[
20|\ell|^2+5m^2+5|n|^2-40t^2=8,
\tag{E6}
]
[
16|\ell|^2-4m^2-|n|^2+4|v|^2=0,
\tag{E7}
]
[
20|\ell|^2+60t^2+5|v|^2=8.
\tag{E8}
]

More explicitly, these are obtained as follows:
[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{associativity identity}&\text{coefficient}&\text{equation}\ \hline
(a^2)b=a(ab)&a,b,\bar b&\text{(E1), (E2), (E3)}\
(ab)b=a(b^2)&a,b&\text{(E4), (E5)}\
(a^2)\bar a=a(a\bar a)&a&\text{(E6)}\
(ab)\bar a=a(b\bar a)&b&\text{(E7)}\
(ab)\bar b=a(b\bar b)&a&\text{(E8)}.
\end{array}
\tag{35}
]

## 4.4. Classification of the possible cubic zeros

We need only the following consequences of (E1)–(E8).

### Case I: (t=0)

Equation (E1) gives (mn=0).

If (m=0), then (E3) gives (n=0). Equations (E6) and (E7) would then say
[
20|\ell|^2=8,
\qquad
16|\ell|^2+4|v|^2=0,
]
which is impossible.

Thus (m\ne0), so (n=0). Equations (E4) and (E3) give
[
\ell=k=0.
]
Finally,
[
m^2=\frac85,
\qquad
|v|^2=m^2.
\tag{36}
]

### Case II: (t>0) and (n=0)

Equation (E1) gives (v=0), and (E2) gives
[
\ell=-\frac m2.
\tag{37}
]
Here (m\ne0), since otherwise (34) would give (a^2=0), contrary to (\sum|a_i|^2=2).

Equations (E3) and (E4) now give
[
k=-6t,
\qquad
m^2=20t^2.
]
Equation (E8) therefore yields
[
t^2=\frac1{20},
\qquad
m^2=1.
\tag{38}
]

### Case III: (t>0) and (n\ne0)

First (m\ne0): otherwise (E1), (E2), and (E3) would successively give
[
v=0,\qquad \ell=0,\qquad n^2=0.
]

Put
[
q=\frac mt,
\qquad
h=\frac{|n|^2}{t^2}>0.
\tag{39}
]
Equations (E1) and (E2) give
[
v=\frac{3mn}{4t},
\qquad
\ell=-\frac{m(32+3h)}{64}.
\tag{40}
]
In particular (\ell) is real.

Equation (E5) gives
[
k=-7m\frac n{\bar n}.
\tag{41}
]
On the other hand, (E4) implies that (k) is real. Hence
[
\sigma:=\frac n{\bar n}\in{1,-1}.
\tag{42}
]
Thus
[
k=-7m\sigma,
\qquad
n^2=\sigma|n|^2.
]

After division by appropriate powers of (t), equations (E4) and (E3) become
[
3hq^2+32q^2-448\sigma q-256=0,
\tag{43}
]
[
-9hq-8\sigma h+112\sigma q^2-96q=0.
\tag{44}
]

Eliminating (h) gives
[
(q-4)(q+2)(21q^2+42q+16)=0
\qquad(\sigma=1),
\tag{45}
]
and
[
(q+4)(q-2)(21q^2-42q+16)=0
\qquad(\sigma=-1).
\tag{46}
]

The roots (q=-2\sigma) give (h=-64) and are impossible. One root of each quadratic also gives
[
h=\frac{-74-14\sqrt{105}}3<0.
]
The candidates (q=4\sigma) give (h=32). But then, from (40) and (E1),
[
\frac{|\ell|^2}{t^2}=64,
\qquad
\frac{|v|^2}{t^2}=288,
]
and the left side of (E7), divided by (t^2), equals
[
16\cdot64-4\cdot16-32+4\cdot288=2080,
]
a contradiction.

The only remaining possibility is
[
q=-\sigma\left(1-\frac{\sqrt{105}}{21}\right),
\qquad
h=\frac{-74+14\sqrt{105}}3.
\tag{47}
]
Equation (E6) then gives
[
t^2=\frac{7(5\sqrt{105}-33)}{1440},
\tag{48}
]
and therefore
[
m^2=\frac{49\sqrt{105}-477}{1080}.
\tag{49}
]

This exhausts all possibilities allowed by the cubic exactness equations.

## 4.5. Evaluation of the quintic transverse functional

For the actual root
[
gu_{ij}
=======

\frac{p_i-p_j}{\sqrt2},
]
we have
[
|z_1|^2W(z)
===========

\frac1{4\sqrt2}
(a_i-a_j)^3(\bar a_i-\bar a_j)(\bar b_i-\bar b_j).
\tag{50}
]
Define
[
\Sigma
======

\sum_{i<j}X(g)_{ij}
(a_i-a_j)^3(\bar a_i-\bar a_j)(\bar b_i-\bar b_j).
\tag{51}
]
Then
[
\Lambda_g(H)=\frac1{4\sqrt2}\operatorname{Im}\Sigma.
\tag{52}
]

In the notation of wedges,
[
X(g)=\frac i2\bigl(\bar a\wedge a+2\bar b\wedge b\bigr).
\tag{53}
]
Indeed, the (ij)-entry of the right side is
[
-\operatorname{Im}(\bar a_i a_j+2\bar b_i b_j)
=\langle p_i,Jp_j\rangle.
]

Modulo an exact matrix, expansion of the degree-five edge expression in (51) gives
[
\begin{aligned}
F\equiv{}&
\bar b\wedge(a^3\bar a)
+\bar a\wedge(a^3\bar b)
-(\bar a\bar b)\wedge a^3\
&+3a\wedge(a^2\bar a\bar b)
-3(a\bar b)\wedge(a^2\bar a)\
&-3(a\bar a)\wedge(a^2\bar b)
+3(a\bar a\bar b)\wedge a^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{54}
]
The omitted exact term is
[
(a^3\bar a\bar b)\wedge\mathbf1,
]
which is annihilated by (X(g)).

For the bilinear dot product (x\cdot y=\sum_ix_iy_i), one has
[
\sum_{i<j}
(u\wedge v)*{ij}(x\wedge y)*{ij}
================================

(u\cdot x)(v\cdot y)-(u\cdot y)(v\cdot x).
\tag{55}
]
Every dot product in (55) is determined by (34): it is five times the coefficient of (\mathbf1) in the corresponding coordinatewise product.

Substituting (34), (53), and (54) into (55) gives
[
\Sigma=-\frac i{80},\mathcal B_{\rm raw},
\tag{56}
]
where
[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal B_{\rm raw}={}&
-960\bar k,\ell t
+160|\ell|^2m
+320\ell t^2
-120m^3\
&-75m|n|^2
-800mt^2
+100m|v|^2
+192m
-160nt\bar v.
\end{aligned}
\tag{57}
]

In each of Cases I–III, (\ell) is real. The conjugates of (E4) and (E1) give
[
\bar k,t=\ell m+4t^2,
\qquad
nt\bar v=\frac34m|n|^2.
\tag{58}
]
Using (58), followed by (E6) and (E8), expression (57) simplifies to
[
\boxed{;
\mathcal B
==========

m\bigl(180m^2+105|n|^2-4400t^2-128\bigr)
-3520\ell t^2.
;}
\tag{59}
]

We now evaluate (59) in the three cases.

In Case I,
[
t=n=\ell=0,\qquad m^2=\frac85,
]
so
[
\mathcal B=160m\ne0.
\tag{60}
]

In Case II,
[
n=0,\qquad t^2=\frac1{20},\qquad
m^2=1,\qquad \ell=-\frac m2,
]
and hence
[
\mathcal B=-80m\ne0.
\tag{61}
]

In Case III, substitution of (40), (47), (48), and (49) gives
[
\mathcal B
==========

60m(75-7\sqrt{105}).
\tag{62}
]
Here (m\ne0), and
[
75-7\sqrt{105}>0.
]
Thus (62) is nonzero.

Equations (52) and (56) now give
[
\Lambda_g(H)
============

-\frac{\mathcal B}{320\sqrt2}\ne0.
\tag{63}
]
This proves Lemma 4. ∎

# 5. A small perturbation removes every zero

We finish the proof of the theorem.

Suppose, contrary to the assertion, that there is no (\varepsilon *0). Then there are nonzero numbers
[
\varepsilon*\nu\longrightarrow0
]
and rotations (g_\nu\in SO(4)) such that the labels of
[
f_{\varepsilon_\nu}=Q+\varepsilon_\nu H
]
at the roots (g_\nu u_{ij}) are exact.

Equivalently, by Lemma 2,
[
\Pi Y_Q(g_\nu)\Pi
+
\varepsilon_\nu\Pi Y_H(g_\nu)\Pi
=0.
\tag{64}
]

After passing to a subsequence, compactness of (SO(4)) gives
[
g_\nu\longrightarrow g.
]
Since (Y_H) is bounded on the compact group, (64) implies
[
\Pi Y_Q(g)\Pi=0.
\tag{65}
]
Thus the (Q)-labels at (gu_{ij}) are exact.

On the other hand, exactness of the (f_{\varepsilon_\nu})-labels and (12) give
[
0
=

\Lambda_{g_\nu}(Y_Q(g_\nu))
+
\varepsilon_\nu\Lambda_{g_\nu}(Y_H(g_\nu)).
]
The first term is zero for every (g_\nu) by Lemma 3. Since (\varepsilon_\nu\ne0),
[
\Lambda_{g_\nu}(Y_H(g_\nu))=0.
]
Passing to the limit,
[
\Lambda_g(Y_H(g))=0.
\tag{66}
]

But (65) and Lemma 4 imply
[
\Lambda_g(Y_H(g))\ne0,
]
contradicting (66).

Therefore an (\varepsilon *0>0) exists, and every (f*\varepsilon) in (1) with
[
0<|\varepsilon|<\varepsilon _0
]
is a counterexample. ∎

## A harmonic refinement

The counterexample may be chosen to have only spherical-harmonic degrees (3) and (5).

The cubic
[
R(z)=\operatorname{Im}(z_1^2\bar z_2)
]
is harmonic. In (\mathbb R^4),
[
\Delta\bigl(|z_1|^2R\bigr)=12R,
\qquad
\Delta\bigl((|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2)R\bigr)=20R.
]
Consequently
[
H_5(z)
======

\left(
|z_1|^2-\frac35(|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2)
\right)R(z)
\tag{67}
]
is a homogeneous harmonic quintic.

Lemma 3 also gives (\Lambda_g(R)=0) for every (g), so replacing (H) by (H_5) does not change the transverse functional. Hence, for all sufficiently small nonzero (\varepsilon),
[
\boxed{;
\widetilde f_\varepsilon(z)
===========================

\operatorname{Re}(z_1^2\bar z_2)
+
\varepsilon
\left(|z_1|^2-\frac35\right)
\operatorname{Im}(z_1^2\bar z_2),
\quad z\in S^3,
;}
\tag{68}
]
is also a counterexample.

Thus degree (5) is already enough to destroy the conclusion, whereas the attached argument proves that all functions with only degrees (1) and (3) satisfy it.
