# Status of the unrestricted problem

Write (m=d+1) and normalize the roots:
[
V=\mathbf 1^\perp\subset \mathbb R^m,\qquad
u_{ij}:=\frac{e_i-e_j}{\sqrt2}\in S(V),\qquad 0\le i<j\le d .
]
This normalization is necessary because (f) is defined on the unit sphere.

The assertion for arbitrary odd (f) is a known open problem: it is the function-theoretic formulation of **Makeev’s conjecture** for the dual difference body of a regular simplex. Kuperberg proved the equivalence with the constant-width-body problem and proved the result in dimension (3). He also showed that the Euler-class obstruction used in dimension (3) vanishes in every dimension (d\ge4). ([NYJM][1])

The Handbook of Discrete and Computational Geometry continues to state the odd-function formulation as a conjecture and notes that the corresponding homological count is even—and the primary equivariant obstruction disappears—for (d\ge4). A later monograph on constant-width bodies likewise reports confirmation only in dimension (3). I found no subsequent published resolution through July 2026. ([California State University, Northridge][2])

Consequently, a complete proof for all smooth odd functions in even the first case (d=4) would settle an open conjecture. What follows are several completely proved positive results, valid for every (d), including a fairly large class of non-zonal functions and a (C^0)-open set of arbitrary odd functions.

# 1. The test-map reduction

Let (\mathcal E) be the vector space of antisymmetric edge-labels
[
y=(y_{ij})*{0\le i,j\le d},\qquad y*{ji}=-y_{ij},\qquad y_{ii}=0.
]
Thus (\dim \mathcal E=\binom m2).

For (c=(c_0,\dots,c_d)\in V), define
[
(\delta c)_{ij}:=\frac{c_i-c_j}{\sqrt2}.
]
These are precisely the restrictions of linear functions to the roots.

## Lemma 1 — Exact edge labels

For (y\in\mathcal E), the following are equivalent.

1. There is (c\in V) such that
   [
   y_{ij}=\frac{c_i-c_j}{\sqrt2}
   \quad\text{for all }i,j.
   ]
2. Every triangle sum vanishes:
   [
   y_{ij}+y_{jk}+y_{ki}=0
   \quad\text{for all distinct }i,j,k.
   ]
3. For every antisymmetric (m\times m) matrix (X) satisfying
   [
   X\mathbf1=0,
   ]
   one has
   [
   \sum_{i<j}X_{ij}y_{ij}=0.
   ]

### Proof

The implication (1\Rightarrow2) follows by telescoping.

Conversely, assume 2. Set (c_0=0) and
[
c_i:=\sqrt2,y_{i0}.
]
The triangle relation for ((i,j,0)) gives
[
y_{ij}+y_{j0}+y_{0i}=0,
]
hence
[
y_{ij}
=y_{i0}-y_{j0}
=\frac{c_i-c_j}{\sqrt2}.
]
Subtracting the mean of the (c_i)'s does not change their differences and places (c) in (V).

To prove the equivalence with 3, observe that
[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i<j}X_{ij}(c_i-c_j)
&=\sum_i c_i\sum_jX_{ij}.
\end{aligned}
]
Thus every matrix (X) with (X\mathbf1=0) is orthogonal to every exact edge-label. Conversely, the exact edge-label space has dimension (m-1=d), while
[
\mathcal C:={X:X^T=-X,\ X\mathbf1=0}
]
has dimension
[
\binom m2-(m-1)=\binom d2.
]
Hence (\mathcal C) is exactly the orthogonal complement of the exact-label space. ∎

The space (\mathcal C) is naturally the Lie algebra (\mathfrak{so}(V)): extend a skew operator on (V) by zero on (\mathbb R\mathbf1).

For an odd continuous (f), define
[
y^f(A)*{ij}:=f(Au*{ij}),\qquad A\in SO(V),
]
and let
[
\mathcal T_f(A):=\operatorname{proj}_{\mathcal C}y^f(A)\in\mathcal C.
]

## Corollary 2 — Test-map formulation

The desired conclusion holds for (f) if and only if
[
\mathcal T_f(A)=0
]
for some (A\in SO(V)).

### Proof

If (\mathcal T_f(A)=0), Lemma 1 gives (c\in V) such that
[
f(Au_{ij})=c\cdot u_{ij}.
]
Taking (b=Ac) gives
[
c\cdot u_{ij}=Ac\cdot Au_{ij}=b\cdot Au_{ij}.
]
The converse is immediate. ∎

Notice the dimension equality
[
\dim SO(d)=\binom d2=\dim\mathcal C.
]
This is the source of the degree/Euler-class approach.

Under the rotation group of the (A_d) root system, (\mathcal T_f) becomes a section of a rank-(\binom d2) bundle over a finite quotient of (SO(d)). The target representation is the adjoint representation twisted by the character recording global root inversion. Since the adjoint factor extends to (SO(d)), this bundle is a direct sum of copies of a line bundle. Kuperberg’s calculation shows that its Euler class is nonzero for (d=3) but vanishes for (d\ge4). This explains precisely why the known topological proof stops at dimension (3); it neither proves nor disproves the assertion in higher dimensions. ([NYJM][1])

# 2. A complete theorem for every odd cubic

The following result holds in every dimension, including all (d>3).

## Theorem 3 — Odd polynomials of degree at most three

Let (d\ge2). Suppose
[
f(x)=P(x)+\ell(x),\qquad x\in S(V),
]
where (P) is an arbitrary homogeneous cubic polynomial on (V) and (\ell) is linear. Then there exist (A\in SO(V)) and (b\in V) such that
[
f(Au_{ij})=b\cdot Au_{ij}
\qquad\text{for every }i<j.
]

Equivalently, the conjecture holds for every odd function whose spherical-harmonic expansion has only degrees (1) and (3).

## Proof

Define the distinguished cubic
[
p(x):=\sum_{i=0}^{d}x_i^3,\qquad x=(x_0,\dots,x_d)\in V.
]

We first establish a tensor identity.

Every cubic (Q) on (V) has a unique symmetric trilinear polarization (T_Q) satisfying
[
Q(x)=T_Q(x,x,x).
]
Extend (T_Q) to (\mathbb R^m) by projecting each argument orthogonally onto (V). Write
[
(T_Q)*{abc}:=T_Q(e_a,e_b,e_c).
]
Equip the space of cubics with the (SO(V))-invariant tensor inner product
[
\langle Q,R\rangle
:=\sum*{a,b,c=0}^{d}(T_Q)*{abc}(T_R)*{abc}.
]

Let (SO(V)) act on cubics by
[
(g\cdot Q)(x):=Q(g^{-1}x).
]
For (X\in\mathfrak{so}(V)), the infinitesimal action on (p) is
[
\begin{aligned}
(X\cdot p)(x)
&=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|*{t=0}p(e^{-tX}x)\
&=-3\sum*{i,j}X_{ij}x_i^2x_j.
\end{aligned}
]
Consequently,
[
\langle Q,X\cdot p\rangle
=-3\sum_{i,j}X_{ij}(T_Q)_{iij}.
\tag{2.1}
]

On the other hand,
[
Q(e_i-e_j)
=(T_Q)*{iii}
-3(T_Q)*{iij}
+3(T_Q)*{ijj}
-(T_Q)*{jjj}.
]
Therefore
[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i<j}X_{ij}Q(e_i-e_j)
&=\frac12\sum_{i,j}X_{ij}
\bigl((T_Q)*{iii}
-3(T_Q)*{iij}\
&\hspace{40mm}
+3(T_Q)*{ijj}
-(T_Q)*{jjj}\bigr).
\end{aligned}
]
The terms containing ((T_Q)*{iii}) and ((T_Q)*{jjj}) vanish because (X\mathbf1=0). Moreover,
[
\sum_{i,j}X_{ij}(T_Q)*{ijj}
=-\sum*{i,j}X_{ij}(T_Q)*{iij},
]
by interchanging (i) and (j), using (X*{ji}=-X_{ij}) and the symmetry of (T_Q). Hence
[
\sum_{i<j}X_{ij}Q(e_i-e_j)
=-3\sum_{i,j}X_{ij}(T_Q)*{iij}.
]
Together with (2.1), this gives the central identity
[
\boxed{;
\langle Q,X\cdot p\rangle
=\sum*{i<j}X_{ij}Q(e_i-e_j)
=2\sqrt2\sum_{i<j}X_{ij}Q(u_{ij}).
;}
\tag{2.2}
]

Now consider the continuous function
[
\Psi:SO(V)\longrightarrow\mathbb R,
\qquad
\Psi(A):=\langle P,A\cdot p\rangle.
]
Since (SO(V)) is compact, (\Psi) has a maximum at some (A_0).

For every (X\in\mathfrak{so}(V)),
[
\begin{aligned}
0
&=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}
\Psi(A_0e^{tX})\
&=\langle P,A_0\cdot(X\cdot p)\rangle\
&=\langle A_0^{-1}\cdot P,X\cdot p\rangle,
\end{aligned}
]
where the last equality uses invariance of the tensor inner product.

Put
[
Q:=A_0^{-1}\cdot P.
]
With our action convention,
[
Q(x)=P(A_0x).
]
By (2.2),
[
\sum_{i<j}X_{ij}P(A_0u_{ij})=0
\qquad
\text{for every }X\in\mathcal C.
]
Lemma 1 therefore gives (c\in V) such that
[
P(A_0u_{ij})
=\frac{c_i-c_j}{\sqrt2}
=c\cdot u_{ij}.
]
Thus, with (b_P=A_0c),
[
P(A_0u_{ij})=b_P\cdot A_0u_{ij}.
]

Finally, write (\ell(x)=a\cdot x), (a\in V). Then
[
\ell(A_0u_{ij})=a\cdot A_0u_{ij}.
]
Taking
[
b:=A_0c+a
]
gives
[
\begin{aligned}
b\cdot A_0u_{ij}
&=c\cdot u_{ij}+a\cdot A_0u_{ij}\
&=P(A_0u_{ij})+\ell(A_0u_{ij})\
&=f(A_0u_{ij}),
\end{aligned}
]
as required. ∎

### Remark

This proof is genuinely dimension-independent. The key fact is the exact variational identity (2.2): the cycle defect of a cubic is the derivative of its correlation with the symmetric cubic (p=\sum x_i^3).

# 3. An open set of arbitrary odd functions

The cubic theorem is stable: the property is not confined to polynomials.

## Theorem 4 — Uniform stability near (\sum x_i^3)

For every (d\ge2), there exists (\varepsilon_d>0) such that every continuous odd
[
f:S(V)\to\mathbb R
]
satisfying
[
\left|f-p\right|*\infty<\varepsilon_d,
\qquad
p(x)=\sum*{i=0}^{d}x_i^3,
]
has the required rotation (A) and linear function (b).

The same conclusion holds in a neighborhood of every nonzero scalar multiple or rotation of (p), and adding an arbitrary linear function does not affect the assertion.

## Proof

For (f\in C_{\mathrm{odd}}(S(V))), let
[
\mathcal T_f(A)
=\operatorname{proj}*{\mathcal C}
\bigl(f(Au*{ij})\bigr)_{i<j}.
]

Since
[
p(u_{ij})
=\left(\frac1{\sqrt2}\right)^3
+\left(-\frac1{\sqrt2}\right)^3
=0,
]
we have
[
\mathcal T_p(I)=0.
]

We calculate the derivative at (I). Let (X\in\mathfrak{so}(V)=\mathcal C). For (u=u_{ij}),
[
dp_u(Xu)
=3\sum_k u_k^2(Xu)*k
=\frac32\bigl((Xu)*i+(Xu)*j\bigr).
]
Now
[
(Xu)*i=-\frac{X*{ij}}{\sqrt2},
\qquad
(Xu)*j=-\frac{X*{ij}}{\sqrt2}.
]
Consequently,
[
dp*{u*{ij}}(Xu*{ij})
=-\frac{3}{\sqrt2}X_{ij}.
]
The resulting edge-label already belongs to (\mathcal C), so
[
D\mathcal T_p(I)[X]
=-\frac3{\sqrt2}X.
\tag{3.1}
]
In particular, (D\mathcal T_p(I)) is an isomorphism.

Choose a sufficiently small ball (B) about (0) in (\mathfrak{so}(V)) such that the exponential map is injective on (\overline B) and the map
[
F_p(X):=\mathcal T_p(e^X)
]
has no zero on (\partial B). By (3.1), its local Brouwer degree at (0) is
[
\deg(F_p,B,0)=\operatorname{sgn}
\det!\left(-\frac3{\sqrt2}I\right)\ne0.
]

Let
[
\eta:=\min_{X\in\partial B}|F_p(X)|>0.
]
If (N=\binom{d+1}{2}), then for every (A),
[
\begin{aligned}
|\mathcal T_f(A)-\mathcal T_p(A)|
&\le
\left(\sum_{i<j}
|f(Au_{ij})-p(Au_{ij})|^2\right)^{1/2}\
&\le \sqrt N,|f-p|*\infty.
\end{aligned}
]
Choose
[
\varepsilon_d<\frac{\eta}{2\sqrt N}.
]
For the straight-line homotopy
[
f_t=p+t(f-p),\qquad 0\le t\le1,
]
the maps (F*{f_t}) remain nonzero on (\partial B). Homotopy invariance of Brouwer degree gives
[
\deg(F_f,B,0)=\deg(F_p,B,0)\ne0.
]
The existence property of the Brouwer degree therefore yields (X\in B) with
[
F_f(X)=0.
]
Taking (A=e^X) and applying Lemma 1 gives the required (b). ∎

Thus, in every dimension (d>3), the set of odd functions satisfying the conclusion contains a nonempty open subset of the entire Banach space (C_{\mathrm{odd}}(S^{d-1})).

# 4. All zonal odd functions

There is another infinite-dimensional class for which the conjecture holds.

## Theorem 5 — Zonal functions

Let (d\ge2), let (a\in S(V)), and let (h:[-1,1]\to\mathbb R) be continuous and odd. Then every function
[
f(x)=h(a\cdot x)+\ell(x),
]
where (\ell) is linear, satisfies the conclusion.

## Proof

It is enough to treat (\ell=0), since a linear summand can simply be added to (b).

Choose any nontrivial partition
[
{0,\dots,d}=S\sqcup T.
]
Let (s=|S|), (t=|T|), so (s+t=m). Define (c\in V) by
[
c_i=
\begin{cases}
\alpha:=\sqrt{\dfrac{t}{sm}},&i\in S,[2mm]
\beta:=-\sqrt{\dfrac{s}{tm}},&i\in T.
\end{cases}
]
Then
[
\sum_i c_i=0,\qquad |c|=1.
]
Since (SO(V)) acts transitively on (S(V)), choose (A\in SO(V)) such that
[
A^Ta=c.
]
Therefore
[
a\cdot Au_{ij}
=c\cdot u_{ij}
=\frac{c_i-c_j}{\sqrt2}.
]

Put
[
\kappa:=\frac{\alpha-\beta}{\sqrt2}>0.
]
The quantity (a\cdot Au_{ij}) is:

* (0) if (i,j) lie in the same part;
* (\kappa) if (i\in S,j\in T);
* (-\kappa) if (i\in T,j\in S).

Since (h) is odd,
[
f(Au_{ij})=
\begin{cases}
0,&i,j\text{ in the same part},\
h(\kappa),&i\in S,j\in T,\
-h(\kappa),&i\in T,j\in S.
\end{cases}
]
Define (z\in\mathbb R^m) by
[
z_i=
\begin{cases}
\sqrt2,h(\kappa),&i\in S,\
0,&i\in T.
\end{cases}
]
After subtracting its mean, we may assume (z\in V), without changing any difference (z_i-z_j). Then
[
f(Au_{ij})=\frac{z_i-z_j}{\sqrt2}=z\cdot u_{ij}.
]
Taking (b=Az) proves
[
f(Au_{ij})=b\cdot Au_{ij}.
]
∎

# Conclusion

The unrestricted assertion for every smooth odd (f) remains open for every (d>3); in particular, there is no currently verified complete proof for (d=4). The primary Euler-class method is known to fail there, but its failure does not produce a counterexample because arbitrary nowhere-zero sections of the test bundle need not arise from evaluating one scalar function on rotated roots. ([NYJM][1])

The complete results proved above are:

[
\boxed{\text{The assertion holds in every dimension for all odd cubics plus linear functions.}}
]

[
\boxed{\text{It holds on a nonempty (C^0)-open set of arbitrary odd functions in every dimension.}}
]

[
\boxed{\text{It holds for every continuous zonal odd function, with an arbitrary linear summand.}}
]

The cubic theorem is particularly broad: it covers the full (\operatorname{Sym}^3(V^*)) representation, not merely symmetric or one-variable examples, and its proof uses a dimension-independent variational normal-form argument.
